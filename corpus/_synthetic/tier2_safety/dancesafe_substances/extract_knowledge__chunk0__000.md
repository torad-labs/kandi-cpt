**Ketamine**
Ketamine is a dissociative anesthetic used in human and veterinary medicine. It belongs to a drug class that includes PCP, DXM, and nitrous oxide, characterized by the separation of perception from sensation. Medical-grade ketamine is typically a liquid; for non-injectable use, it is evaporated into a white powder for insufflation.

**Pharmacology and Effects**
Low doses produce a trance-like or "floaty" sensation lasting 45 to 60 minutes, with a full return to baseline within 1.5 to 2 hours. Higher doses induce hallucinogenic and dissociative effects, including out-of-body experiences known as "k-holes." Insufflated doses typically range from 30 to 60 mg (bumps/lines), with effects manifesting in 5 to 15 minutes. A dose of 100 mg is generally sufficient to induce a full dissociative state.

**Risks and Contraindications**
While ketamine does not independently suppress heart rate or respiration, combining it with depressants—such as alcohol, benzodiazepines, or GHB—increases the risk of blackouts, vomiting, erratic body temperature, and loss of consciousness. High doses can lead to aspiration of vomit or injury from falls. Long-term use is associated with ketamine cystitis, a condition involving damage to the bladder lining that may cause pelvic pain, incontinence, or necessitate bladder removal.

**Safety and Administration**
Ketamine reduces motor control and pain signaling by disrupting communication between the brain and body. Due to the risk of immobility, individuals using high doses are advised to remain seated or lying down and avoid solitary use. Isomer-specific claims (e.g., "r-ketamine" or "s-ketamine") cannot be verified without advanced laboratory equipment.

**LSD**
LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide), discovered in 1938, is a psychedelic drug with an effect duration of 8 to 12 hours. The experience is often characterized by "chapter switching," where themes or emotions shift abruptly due to environmental changes, specific thoughts, or events.
