### Cardiovascular Risks of Cocaine
Cocaine presents a higher risk of cardiovascular injury compared to other stimulants. High or frequent dosages can result in damage to the heart and blood vessels, potentially leading to aneurysms, strokes, and myocardial infarctions. Some individuals may experience sudden cardiovascular side effects even at lower dosages.

Physiological effects include acute increases in blood pressure, heart rate, and body temperature. When combined with physical exertion or other stimulants, these effects may manifest as hypertension, arrhythmia, and overheating.

### Administration and Complications
Cocaine is a short-acting substance; the subsequent "comedown" period can lead to compulsive redosing, a cycle exacerbated by potent administration routes or increased frequency of use. Repeated intranasal administration can cause damage to the sinuses and nasal cavities, which may be mitigated by the use of saline spray.

Stimulant use often results in insomnia. Sleep deprivation lasting two or more days significantly increases the risk of entering a psychotic state. Furthermore, the sharing of snorting devices facilitates the transmission of diseases, including Hepatitis C. Disposable alternatives include personal straws or rolled paper.

### Drug Checking and Reagent Testing
Drug checking, also referred to as adulterant screening or pill testing, is a harm reduction practice used to identify illicit, pharmaceutical, or inert substances. As of December 2021, over 1,100 novel substances have been reported to the United Nations.

Reagent testing utilizes specific liquid chemicals that produce color changes when they react with certain drugs. For example, the Marquis reagent turns black in the presence of MDA and MDMA, while Simon's reagent turns blue with MDMA and either fails to react or turns muddy gray with MDA.

Reagent tests identify unexpected reactions ("red flags") rather than guaranteeing purity. They cannot provide a full chemical analysis, which requires laboratory testing. A historical example occurred during the MDMA shortage (2009–2017), where pills containing 90% PMMA and 10% MDMA passed Marquis tests because the MDMA reaction masked the non-reaction of PMMA/PMA. Additionally, no reagent test is capable of detecting fentanyl.
