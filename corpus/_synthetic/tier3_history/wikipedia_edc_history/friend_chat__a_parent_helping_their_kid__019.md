Friend: I’m trying to help my kid understand the history of the local tracks. What actually happened with the Rodriguez family back in the late 80s?

Vet: It was a mess. In March 1988, the city council almost revoked the lease because Rodriguez wasn't paying rent and was selling food without sales tax permits. Rodriguez Jr. actually admitted to it, but he blamed it on his dad being out of the country for two years and dealing with a divorce.

Friend: Did they kick them out immediately?

Vet: Not right away. The city approved terminating the lease, but they were allowed to keep operating the track until December 1990. While that was happening, the city decided to sell the land to a private operator on November 17.

Friend: I heard Rodriguez tried to fight the sale. Did that work?

Vet: Not really. He argued that he’d made a ton of improvements to the track, but the city pushed through with the sale. The only catch was that the new buyer had to promise to maintain the track for 10 years.

Friend: So who actually bought it?

Vet: Richie Clyne bought the complex in May 1989 for $1,070,000. He was the director of the automobile museum at the Imperial Palace Hotel—which is the Linq now. He and an investor, G. Robert Diero, wanted to totally revamp the place because they thought it was dangerous and worn out.

Friend: Did they change the name when they took over?

Vet: Yeah, they rebranded it as the Las Vegas International Raceway. But honestly, by early 1990, the place was still known as "old, dirty, and tattered." A writer from the Review-Journal, Greg Bortolin, said it would take millions in renovations to attract big leagues, and since UNLV was the only major sports thing in town back then, there was no guarantee it would work.

Friend: Was it always meant to be a race track?

Vet: Surprisingly, no. Clyne told the media manager, Neal Reid, that he originally wanted to turn the whole area into an industrial park. He only changed his mind after some motorsport businessmen, Bob Bahre and Tom Wheatcroft, convinced him otherwise.

Friend: When did the racing actually pick up under Clyne?

Vet: The first races under his leadership were the Nissan 400—which is what we call the Mint 400 now. By 1991, they were trying to bring in more events and do general renovations.

Friend: I see another name in the records, David Juberg. Where does he fit in?

Vet: In 1993, Juberg and Craig Graham—both amateur racers—took over a lease from Clyne. Juberg renamed the complex the Las Vegas Speedway Park that same year.

Friend: Did they finally get some big events going?

Vet: Not really. In May 1993, they took over the master lease from Tom Villardi, but the track still struggled to land major events. They couldn't even get off-road racing started because of environmental concerns.
