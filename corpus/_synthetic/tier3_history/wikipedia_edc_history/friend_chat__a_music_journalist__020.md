Friend: I’m digging into the origins of the Las Vegas Motor Speedway for a piece. Was it always a sure thing, or was there a fight for the land first?

Vet: Total battle. Back in '93, a guy named Clyne was testing the waters, but by '94, Bruton Smith from SMI actually wanted the spot first. He tried to grab land next to the Las Vegas Speedway Park, but it was owned by Ralph Engelstad of the Imperial Palace. Since they were buddies, Smith actually stepped aside and looked toward Texas instead.

Friend: So if Smith backed out, how did Clyne actually get the project off the ground?

Vet: He went bold. On November 30, 1994, Clyne announced a $65-70 million facility with 100,000 seats designed specifically for the NASCAR Winston Cup Series. He set a hard opening date for April 1, 1996.

Friend: Did NASCAR just hand him a race date immediately?

Vet: Not even close. Mike Helton and the NASCAR brass were basically saying the Cup schedule was already maxed out. Clyne had the track, but he didn't have the guaranteed dates.

Friend: If the Cup series was a "maybe," how did they actually launch the place?

Vet: They pivoted to IndyCar. In May '95, Clyne and Tony George officially announced a 200-mile race to christen the track on September 15, 1996. That was the real anchor.

Friend: What did the actual footprint look like? Was it just the oval?

Vet: Way bigger. In January '95, he confirmed a 1,100-acre plot along Interstate 15. He even bundled in a 40-acre industrial park as part of the original blueprint.

Friend: When did it actually start feeling like a reality?

Vet: August '95. Construction kicked off then, and by November, they added NASCAR Craftsman Truck Series races to the mix. Everything was moving right on schedule.

Friend: I heard there was some corporate shuffling toward the end. Who actually owned the thing?

Vet: It got messy. In April '96, Clyne bought out Juberg’s share of the Speedway Park to consolidate everything under the LVMS name. Then, Bruton Smith leaked that Engelstad was actually funding the majority of it, though Clyne played it coy and never confirmed it.

Friend: Did any other big Vegas names jump in before the doors opened?

Vet: Yeah, Bill Bennett, the owner of the Sahara Hotel, joined as a part-owner in September '96. Even though the track was basically ready, Clyne announced another five-year construction plan that same month to really polish the facility.

Friend: Was the economic impact as big as they claimed?

Vet: The projections were wild. By February '96, they were estimating the track would inject over $890 million into the Las Vegas economy.
