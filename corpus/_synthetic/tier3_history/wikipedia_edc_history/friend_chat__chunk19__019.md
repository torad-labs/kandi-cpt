Friend: No way. Why would they risk the whole venue over food permits?

Vet: It was a mess. Rodriguez Jr. eventually admitted it, but he blamed his dad, who had been out of the country for two years and was dealing with a divorce. The city terminated the lease, though they let them keep operating until December 1990.

Friend: So how did it become the massive complex we have now?

Vet: The city decided to sell the land to a private operator on November 17. Rodriguez tried to argue that he’d made too many improvements to lose it, but the sale went through anyway—as long as the buyer promised to maintain the track for 10 years.

Friend: Who ended up buying it?

Vet: Richie Clyne. He was the director of the Imperial Palace auto museum. He and an investor, G. Robert Diero, dropped $1,070,000 for it in May '89. They renamed it the Las Vegas International Raceway.

Friend: Was it a hit right away?

Vet: Far from it. By 1990, everyone called it "old, dirty, and tattered." A writer for the Review-Journal, Greg Bortolin, said it would take millions in renovations just to get major leagues interested, and back then, UNLV was the only big game in town.

Friend: Did Clyne actually want to run a venue?

Vet: Honestly? No. He told the media manager, Neal Reid, that he originally wanted to turn the spot into an industrial park! He only stuck with it because Bob Bahre and Tom Wheatcroft convinced him otherwise.

Friend: So when did the actual events start?

Vet: He kicked things off with the Nissan 400—which we know now as the Mint 400. By '91, he was trying to expand and renovate, but then David Juberg and Craig Graham took over the lease in '93.

Friend: I think I've heard of Juberg. Did he change things?

Vet: He did. He renamed the whole thing the Las Vegas Speedway Park in '93 and took over the master lease from Tom Villardi in May. 

Friend: Did that finally bring in the big crowds?
