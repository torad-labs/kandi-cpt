# Las Vegas Motor Speedway

The **Las Vegas Motor Speedway (LVMS)** is a motorsports complex located in Las Vegas, Nevada, situated between Interstate 15 and Las Vegas Boulevard. Since its inception, the facility has expanded from an initial 200 acres to over 1,200 acres as of 2015. While the venue's peak capacity once exceeded 140,000 seats, its stated capacity was 80,000 as of 2023.

## Facilities and Infrastructure

A significant renovation project in 2006 introduced several modern amenities to the complex. These include the **Neon Garage**, an interactive spectator garage accessible via a fee during race weekends, and the **LocaliQ Digital Center**, a three-story, 60,000-square-foot media center located within the track's infield.

## Track Layouts and Stages

The complex comprises several distinct racing venues:

*   **The Bullring:** Originally constructed in 1985 as a 0.33 mi (0.53 km) oval utilizing barriers donated from the Caesars Palace Grand Prix, the track was modified in 2000 to a length of 0.375 mi (0.604 km).
*   **Dirt Oval:** A 0.500 mi (0.805 km) clay dirt oval opened in November 1996, which has hosted events for the World of Outlaws.
*   **The Strip:** The complex's drag racing facility originated in 1972. Following the acquisition of the track by Bruton Smith in 1999, a new 0.250 mi (0.402 km) strip was commissioned, opening in April 2000. The facility was further expanded to four lanes in 2017.

## History

### Planning and Construction
The development of the complex began on August 14, 1970, when businessman Carl "Curly" Price sought to lease land near Nellis Air Force Base from the City of Las Vegas. Price's proposal included a drag strip, a small road course, and an oval track to address the regional lack of professional racing facilities. Construction of the original Las Vegas Motor Speedway was underway by February 1971.

## Logistics and Safety

The complex's proximity to Nellis Air Force Base has influenced its architectural development. A proposal to construct a 127-unit condominium tower overlooking the first turn was blocked following opposition from the United States Air Force. Military officials cited safety risks associated with regular flight training and drills conducted in the vicinity. Despite initial resistance from SMI CEO Bruton Smith, the project was abandoned after the Air Force lobbied the Clark County government to deny the lodging permits.
