### Cardiovascular Risks and Stimulant Use
Cocaine use is associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular injury compared to other stimulants. High or frequent doses can cause damage to blood vessels and the heart, potentially leading to heart attacks, aneurysms, and strokes. In some instances, sudden cardiovascular side effects occur even at lower doses.

Physiological responses to cocaine include rapid increases in heart rate, blood pressure, and body temperature. When combined with physical exertion or other stimulants, users may experience hypertension, arrhythmia (irregular heartbeat), and overheating. These risks are exacerbated by extreme environmental conditions, such as high desert temperatures.

### Administration and Harm Reduction
Cocaine is a short-acting substance. The subsequent "comedown" period can be unpleasant, particularly following high doses or potent administration routes, often leading to compulsive redosing. 

Specific health risks associated with administration include:
*   **Nasal Damage:** Repeated snorting can damage the sinuses and nasal cavities; saline spray is recommended for mitigation.
*   **Sleep Deprivation:** Stimulant use can cause insomnia. Remaining awake for two or more days significantly increases the risk of entering a psychotic state.
*   **Disease Transmission:** Sharing snorting devices can spread Hepatitis C. The use of disposable tools, such as personal straws or rolled paper, is recommended.

### Drug Checking and Adulterant Screening
Drug checking is a harm reduction process used to identify illicit, pharmaceutical, or inert substances in a sample. As of December 2021, the United Nations reported over 1,100 novel substances.

**Reagent Testing**
Reagent testing utilizes liquid chemicals that change color upon reaction with specific drugs. For example, the Marquis reagent turns black in the presence of MDMA and MDA, while Simon’s reagent turns blue with MDMA and either remains unchanged or turns muddy gray with MDA.

Reagent tests are designed to identify "red flags" rather than guarantee purity. They cannot provide a complete chemical analysis, which requires a laboratory. Historical data from the 2009–2017 MDMA shortage shows that substances like PMMA and PMA may not react with Marquis reagents, potentially allowing contaminated pills to pass as pure if a small amount of MDMA is present. Additionally, no reagent test is capable of detecting fentanyl.
